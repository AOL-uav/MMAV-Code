# Bent-up tips beat bigger fins against twist

**Short answer: probably yes, but not the way the question first imagines it.** A small unpowered airframe can be made to give *repeatable* passive flight. It cannot be made to give *straight* flight from an untrimmed, as-built airframe. The reason is that no aerodynamic shape returns a glider to a fixed heading: heading is neutrally stable by the symmetry of the equations of motion ([Etkin & Reid](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Dynamics+of+Flight%3A+Stability+and+Control%2C+3rd+Edition-p-9780471034186)). What design can do is shrink the turn produced by each degree of build error, make sure the spiral converges, and keep the pitch motion away from stall. The steady-turn equation behind the project's AVL study splits into two parts. Turns caused by **rolling** asymmetry (wing-half twist, which the AVL study found dominant) shrink without limit as dihedral effect × yaw damping ÷ weathercock stiffness grows. Turns caused by **yawing** asymmetry (fin toe) can never fall below about 1/|Cnr|, so only yaw damping (fin area × arm²) fights them. For a flat swept flying wing, the strongest passive lever is therefore **dihedral concentrated at the tips**, combined with a **lower trim CL**. A long-arm fin helps more than a bigger fin. Pendulums, low CG and wing flexibility do not cancel a steady built-in turn. Every published gram-scale passive glider programme found split its nominally identical builds between straight glides and spirals, so 12-32 m circles from 0.1-0.3° of twist are normal at this scale, not a project-specific mistake. The persistent pitch swoops are not explained by a linear phugoid, which should have died out within a few cycles. They look like the **stall-limited "bounding" cycle** measured in thin-plate gliders, and the supported cure is moving the CG forward.

## A 0.1-0.3° twist turning the glider is the expected result

The project's AVL turn formula is the textbook steady-turn solution: set the roll and yaw moment equations to zero with a constant asymmetry ΔCl, ΔCn and solve for yaw rate, r̂ = (Clβ·ΔCn − Cnβ·ΔCl)/(Clr·Cnβ − Clβ·Cnr) ([Etkin & Reid](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Dynamics+of+Flight%3A+Stability+and+Control%2C+3rd+Edition-p-9780471034186); [Stevens, Lewis & Johnson](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Aircraft+Control+and+Simulation%3A+Dynamics%2C+Controls+Design%2C+and+Autonomous+Systems%2C+3rd+Edition-p-9781118870983)). The denominator is the spiral-stability term. The usual criterion is Clβ·Cnr − Cnβ·Clr > 0, which in the project's D notation means **D < 0 is spirally stable**. Check that sign convention before comparing configurations ([Phillips, *Mechanics of Flight*](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Mechanics+of+Flight%2C+2nd+Edition-p-9780470539750)).

The research team rearranged this formula. The rearrangement is our derivation from the standard equations, not something any textbook states, so treat it as *model/analysis* until AVL numbers confirm it. Let K = |Clβ|·|Cnr|/Cnβ. Turn per unit rolling asymmetry is then −1/(K − Clr), and **it goes to zero as K grows**. Turn per unit yawing asymmetry is 1/(|Cnr| − Clr·Cnβ/|Clβ|), and **even infinite dihedral only brings it down to 1/|Cnr|**. Three consequences follow, and they match what the project already saw in AVL. First, a fin trim tab can cancel yaw-type asymmetry but not twist, because twist is a ΔCl and its cure is K, not the fin. Second, weathercock stability (Cnβ) sits in the denominator of K, so a stiffer fin on a short arm actually *amplifies* twist-induced turning, because the glider yaws into the sideslip the roll creates. A flying-wing design paper makes the same observation in another form: weak directional stability helps spiral stability ([Hamada 2018](https://www.athensjournals.gr/technology/2018-5-3-2-Hamada.pdf)). Third, wing Clr ≈ CL/4, so flying at high CL erodes the margin. One more coupling matters. Antisymmetric twist on a tapered wing produces an adverse yawing moment along with the rolling moment, so "twist" is never a pure ΔCl ([Hunsaker, Montgomery & Joo 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0954410019886939)). AVL captures this automatically.

Free-flight practice confirms the order of magnitude. A standard F1B (Wakefield) glide circle is set with only **0.5° of wash-in on one main panel** plus a touch of rudder ([Joyner, Model Aviation 2005](https://library.modelaviation.com/article/ff-duration-30)). Those are large models with high dihedral and long tails. A flat, finless or short-arm glider showing 12-32 m circles from 0.1-0.3° is therefore consistent with hobby experience, not anomalous. Free-flighters do not design warps away. They steam them out, pin the wing on a shim, and re-test ([Model Aviation, FF Old Timers](https://library.modelaviation.com/article/free-flight-old-timers-45)). Horten wrote in 1949 that building-error losses in small tailless gliders "will not be able to be avoided without using special assembling and checking devices," and he built in extra twist to compensate ([Horten 1949](https://www.rcsoaringdigest.com/TWITT/Horten_Oct_49.html)).

The closest published systems hit the same wall. Kim et al.'s 25 cm PCB flying-wing sensor gliders use heat-shrink hinges with a **4° (1σ) fold-angle scatter**. In their Monte Carlo, most units with slightly different left/right elevon angles "descended in a turning pattern" ([Kim et al., arXiv 2603.18861](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18861)). Wiesemüller et al.'s 1.25-1.75 g Alsomitra-shaped wafer-paper gliders, released from a gripper that imitated a drone, showed two modes indoors, straight glide and downward spiral. The authors blamed "slight variations in manufacturing or warping," and found **little dependence on release angle** between 25° and 55° nose-down ([Wiesemüller et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2022.1011793/full)). Both groups ended up treating the spiral as useful for spreading sensors out. A robot that folded and flew about 500 paper planes found that nominally identical designs land in discrete, probabilistic behaviour classes ([Obayashi et al. 2023](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/295813)). Nature's own finless glider behaves the same way: **11 of 15** tracked *Alsomitra* seeds descended helically ([Certini 2023](https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/39840)). The research team found **no** peer-reviewed study that flew a batch of identical passive micro gliders and reported heading scatter, and no Monte Carlo of build tolerance against lateral trim for any small UAV. The project's AVL sensitivity study and its drop log appear to be ahead of the published literature.

## Concentrated tip dihedral leads the ranked design levers

The ranking below targets robustness to wing-half twist first, because that is what the project's AVL found dominant. Tags follow the brief: **[M]** measured/peer-reviewed, **[A]** model/analysis, **[H]** hobbyist practice, **[S]** speculation.

| Rank | Lever | What it does | Evidence | Main cost or caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dihedral concentrated outboard (bent-up tips, polyhedral) | Raises Clβ, and so K; the outer half of the semispan carries ≈65 % of the roll moment | [A] Rawdon EDA weighting; [A] frigatebird study: dihedral is "the key factor" for spiral convergence; [M] finless frigatebird-model flight stayed laterally convergent; [H] free-flight dihedral 5-15° | Erodes dutch-roll damping (project AVL); no tailless calibration |
| 2 | Lower trim CL (CG forward, fly faster) | Shrinks Clr ≈ CL/4; raises Rawdon SS ∝ 1/CL; keeps tips away from stall; also helps pitch | [A] textbook; [A]/[H] Rawdon; [H] Tutor sailplane flew straight at CL 0.4 but circled at 0.9 | Higher sink rate and speed; more pull-out height |
| 3 | Per-airframe trim step: one roll-type bendable tab set by a hand or hill glide | Cancels each unit's actual twist | [H] universal free-flight practice; [H] F1E, the only straight-flying class, still needs it | Not "design-only"; geometry drifts with humidity and storage |
| 4 | Build for symmetry: hard stops, jigs, equal washout | Shrinks ΔCl at the source | [M] Kim: 4° scatter in elastic hinges; [H] Horten jigs; [H] equal washout or "a roll effect is created" | Tolerance cost; does not reduce sensitivity |
| 5 | Long fin arm (boom, or a tail with a vertical element) rather than a bigger fin | Cnr ∝ S_v·l_v² while Cnβ ∝ S_v·l_v, so |Cnr|/Cnβ ≈ 2·l_v/b; also sets the floor for yaw-type asymmetry | [A] textbook strip theory; [H] free-flight fin arm 35-100 % of span | Adds a structure; fin effectiveness at Re ~5-10k unknown |
| 6 | Up-canted tip fins | Fin side force above the CG adds stabilising Clβ; cant trades Cnβ (≈cos²) for Clβ | [A] textbook; [H] tip-fin toe-in practice "debatable" | Short arm gives little Cnr; tip fins lower flutter speed on flexible wings [M] |
| 7 | Symmetric, generous washout | Keeps both tips far from stall, so a tiny asymmetry does not decide which tip stalls first | [H] Foote 1956, Horten 1949 | Lift and L/D cost |
| 8 | Fin or rudder trim tab | Cancels yaw-type asymmetry (fin toe) | [A] project AVL; [H] "avoid rudder for glide trim, it is speed-sensitive" | Does nothing for twist; speed sensitivity matters on a nose-down drop |
| 9 | Ventral fin | Adds Cnβ and Cnr but *reduces* Clβ | [A] textbook sign argument | Use only if dutch roll, not spiral, limits the design |
| 10 | Wing flexibility / aeroelastic washout | To first order scales the asymmetric load and the restoring Clβ equally, so no net gain | [M] Florida MAVs: gust attenuation, not asymmetry; [S] the cancellation argument | Untested; may help only with load-relieving trailing edges |
| 11 | Pendulum devices | Senses sideslip, not bank; centred by centrifugal force in a steady turn | [A] physics; [H] Haight 1980: "stuck pendulum" failure | Cannot remove a steady circle; mass and friction at 8-25 g |
| 12 | Magnetic compass rudder (F1E) | The only known passive mechanism giving a true heading reference | [H] FAI F1E since 1951/1977 | Mass at gram scale unknown; not a stability fix |

Effective dihedral leads because it is the only geometric knob that moves the twist sensitivity directly with no theoretical floor. Rawdon defines effective dihedral angle (EDA) by weighting each panel's dihedral by its share of the roll moment. With a break at mid-semispan, the outer panel counts 0.65 and the inner 0.35, so a flat centre with 10° tips already gives EDA 6.5° ([Beron-Rawdon, Dihedral Part 1](https://library.modelaviation.com/article/dihedral)). He calls a tip-to-main dihedral ratio of about 3 "near-optimal," at a tiny cost: a 10° V loses about 0.6 % of best L/D ([Dihedral Part 4](https://library.modelaviation.com/article/dihedral-3)). The bird evidence agrees. Across 9,724 dihedral distributions on a frigatebird model, every case had a convergent dutch roll, but only **60.7 %** had a convergent spiral, and dihedral distribution decided which. A finless bungee-launched model of that bird glided hands-off and stayed laterally convergent ([Wei et al. 2022](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/AEAT-12-2021-0364/full/html)). The ceiling is dutch roll. A large Clβ/Cnβ ratio lowers dutch-roll damping, which is exactly the erosion the project's AVL found with centre dihedral. Putting the dihedral at the tips buys more Clβ per degree than centre dihedral does, but it does not remove that trade. No small-glider dutch-roll damping criterion exists. A target of ζ ≈ 0.1-0.2 is borrowed from manned-aircraft practice [S].

The fin story is subtler than "add fin." On a swept flying wing, tip fins sit only about 0.1-0.2 span behind the CG. A boomed fin, or a bird tail with a vertical element, reaches 0.3-0.5, roughly **2-5× more yaw damping per unit weathercock stiffness** (our arithmetic from the textbook fin derivatives). Raising fin area at a fixed short arm raises Cnβ almost as fast as Cnr, so K barely moves. It also strengthens the spiral tendency, which is the free-flight rule "the larger the vertical tail is, the more wing dihedral is needed" ([Model Aviation 1992](https://library.modelaviation.com/article/vertical-tail-size-models-1); [DeLoach 2020](https://www.modelaviation.com/node/2076)). For B45 with tip fins, the practical reading is to hold **fin-toe tolerance tighter than twist tolerance**, because the short arm gives the fins little power to reject their own misalignment. For Swift A as flown, which had no vertical surface at all, it has only sweep-generated Cnβ and very little Cnr, so it is highly sensitive to any yaw-type error. A twisted tail is exactly how birds steer in yaw ([Physics Today on Chang et al. 2024](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/birdlike-robot-flies-steady-without-a-vertical-tail)), so a bent-up tail with uneven bends is a prime suspect for Swift's turning [S].

## Five rules of thumb, each with a stated range of validity

| Rule | Value | Source and tag | Fit to this project |
|---|---|---|---|

| EDA weighting, break at mid-semispan | Outer panel 0.65, inner 0.35 | [Rawdon 1988](https://library.modelaviation.com/article/dihedral) [A] | Directly usable for bent-up tips |
| Vertical tail volume V_v = S_v·l_v/(S·b) | ≈0.02 sailplanes, ≈0.04 light aircraft; free-flight fin 5-8 % of wing area, arm 35-45 % of span | [Raymer](https://arc.aiaa.org/doi/book/10.2514/4.104909) [A]; [Rawdon Part 4](https://library.modelaviation.com/article/dihedral-3), [DeLoach](https://www.modelaviation.com/node/2076) [H] | No data below Re 30k; small scale tolerates lower V_v ([Sachs 2007](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.20175)) |
| Fin damping-to-stiffness ratio | Cnr,fin/Cnβ,fin ≈ −2·l_v/b | Textbook strip theory [A] | Doubling the arm doubles the twist-rejection gain at fixed Cnβ |
| Tailless directional minimum | Cnβ ≥ ~0.002 /deg judged necessary | NASA free-flight tunnel tailless glider ([NTRS 20090026465](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20090026465)) [M, snippet only] | Large-scale model; small scale may need less |
| Steady-glide CG window for thin plates | Bounding at CoM 0.14 of chord, gliding 0.22-0.29, diving 0.31 | [Li et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2022.89) [M] | Measured at Re 10³-10⁴ on tailless plates |
| Hand-launch glider static margin | ~15 % | [McKiou, NAR](https://nar.org/?p=2840) [H] | Tailed models; a starting point only |

The Rawdon rule shows the size of the problem plainly. For a flat wing (EDA 0°) it gives SS ≈ 0, so any spiral stability must come from sweep, tip-fin height and tip drag. Applied out of its calibration range to a flying wing with l_v/b ≈ 0.15, reaching SS 5.7 needs **EDA ≈ 23° at CL 0.6 but only ≈13° at CL 0.35** (our arithmetic). That pairing is why levers 1 and 2 are listed together. At the CL 0.3-0.4 trim the pitch notes assume for a 400 mm, 10 g glider, about 20° of bend on the outer half of each semispan would reach that EDA, which is buildable. Treat the number as a direction, not a design value, because no SS calibration exists for tailless models. Rawdon's own flight test shows the CL dependence directly: his Tutor sailplane at 5.7° EDA rolled into a circle at about 20° bank at CL ≈ 0.9, yet flew straight at CL ≈ 0.4 ([Beron-Rawdon 1990](https://library.modelaviation.com/article/spiral-stability-and-bowl-effect)).

## The sources disagree on pendulums, birds and AVL's accuracy

**Pendulum and low-CG folklore versus physics.** The FAA handbook and many hobby sources still teach a "keel" or pendulum effect ([RC Scale Builder](https://www.rcscalebuilder.com/Tutorials/dihedral/dihedral.pdf)). Wind-tunnel and analytical sources attribute the high-wing advantage to wing-fuselage interference, not gravity ([Kitplanes, Wainfan](https://www.kitplanes.com/wind-tunnel-135/); [NACA via NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19930091107/downloads/19930091107.pdf)). Physics favours the second view. Gravity acts at the CG, so a low CG creates no restoring torque by itself. A low CG only gives sideslip-generated side forces (on fins, keels, bodies) a vertical lever arm. Thomas & Taylor's view that pendulum stability matters more for animals than for aircraft ([Thomas & Taylor 2001](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:39c37f27-3793-45e2-9b0e-156495ace658)) is best read in that sense. Pendulum-*actuated* controls fail for a related reason. In a coordinated turn the apparent gravity lines up with the body's vertical axis, so the pendulum hangs centred, exactly like a slip ball. Haight's pendulum installations on scale free-flight models suffered this "centrifugal force pulls the pendulum toward neutral" failure. He warned that rudder pendulums can over-correct into a spiral ([Haight 1980](https://library.modelaviation.com/article/pits-and-pendulum)). At best a pendulum equals a little more fin or dihedral, and it cannot remove a steady circle. A hanging parafoil payload is a different case: it works only because canopy and payload are separate bodies.

**Are birds passively stable?** Sachs's analyses say small fliers need very little static yaw stability, because yaw inertia shrinks faster with size than the restoring moment, and the wing alone can supply yaw damping ([Sachs 2007](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/why-birds-and-miniscale-airplanes-need-no-vertical-tail/)). Against that, the finless PigeonBot II had a dutch-roll *instability* that it damped only with reflexive wing and tail morphing ([Chang, Chin & Lentink 2024](https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/bird-inspired-reflexive-morphing-enables-rudderless-flight)). Analyses of measured owl and peregrine shapes found dutch-roll and spiral stability varying from flight to flight ([Durston et al. 2019](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/avian-surface-reconstruction-in-free-flight-with-application-to-f/)). Our reading reconciles them [S]: swept, dihedralled wings (Sachs, Wei) converge, while a comparatively unswept pigeon wing does not. The transferable recipe is outer-wing sweep for Cnβ *plus* dihedral for the spiral. Neither alone suffices, and bird-like geometry by itself guarantees nothing.

**How far to trust AVL at Re 10-30k.** Measured roll-due-to-sideslip of thin low-aspect-ratio plates departs from vortex-lattice predictions, because tip-vortex roll-up at low Re is *destabilising* in Clβ ([Linehan & Mohseni, AIAA J.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10097585)). The Weissinger vortex arrangement used by lifting-line and VLM codes is inconsistent with the flow over low-AR wings with large tip chords ([NSF PAR 10097590](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10097590)), and MAV cross-coupling derivatives are as large as the primary ones ([APS DFD 2010](https://archive.aps.org/dfd/2010/ex/6)). The finless frigatebird model's measured dutch roll (ωn 1.87 rad/s, ζ 0.445) differed sharply from theory (2.63 rad/s, ζ 0.179) ([Wei et al. 2022](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/AEAT-12-2021-0364/full/html)). Use AVL to **rank** configurations, not to predict circle radii, until a flight calibration exists. Lift slope is similarly uncertain. Thin aluminium plates at Re 1.2-1.5×10⁴ measured near or above theory, 0.066-0.078 /deg ([Okamoto & Jinba 2009](https://akita.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/927/files/KJ00005254825.pdf)), while a membrane MAV at Re 25k reached only 67 % of the Helmbold prediction ([Uhlig & Selig 2013](https://m-selig.web.engr.illinois.edu/pubs/UhligSelig-2013-JofAC-ViconVapor.pdf)). Treat CLα at Re 17k as uncertain by about ±30 %.

**Flexibility.** The Florida flexible-wing MAVs show that passive "adaptive washout" reduces gust sensitivity ([Waszak & Jenkins 2001](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20010081319/downloads/20010081319.pdf); [Ifju et al. 2002](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20020024082/downloads/20020024082.pdf)). No source shows it cancels built-in asymmetry. Our first-order argument says uniform flexibility scales the asymmetric rolling moment and the lift-based restoring Clβ by the same factor, so the turn per degree of twist stays the same [S]. The exception is a compliant trailing edge that unloads a *bent* trailing-edge tab under pressure, for which no data exist.

**Where evidence is simply absent.** The team found no measured warp-versus-circle-radius data for any small glider, hobby or academic. There is no SS calibration for tailless or tip-fin layouts, no fin-effectiveness data at fin Re ~5-10k, and no isolated measurement of fin cant or CG-height effects on Clβ below Re 50k. Nothing measures how FDM print orientation makes 0.4-0.8 mm plates twist, and nothing gives the mass of an F1E-style magnet rudder at gram scale. The project's own data would be first of its kind on most of these.

## The swoops look like a stall-limited cycle, not a phugoid

Linear theory rules out the obvious explanation. The Lanchester phugoid has ωn ≈ √2·g/V, giving a period of about **2.5 s at 5.5 m/s**. Drag is its only damping, with ζ ≈ 1/(√2·L/D) ([MIT 16.01 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-01-unified-engineering-i-ii-iii-iv-fall-2005-spring-2006/4e932d24f9d4b17918820220ce1bd11e_phugoid.pdf)). The research team did not verify that exact damping formula against the primary text, though it is the commonly quoted form. Thin plates at Re ~1.3×10⁴ reach (L/D)max of only ~5.5-6.5 ([Okamoto & Jinba 2009](https://akita.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/927/files/KJ00005254825.pdf)). That puts ζ near 0.11-0.13, higher than the ~0.09 the project model took from Re-60k data. With that damping, amplitude after 10 cycles is **below 0.3 % of the start**. Swoops still visible after 10 cycles therefore need either a nonlinear mechanism that sustains them or continual excitation from the air.

The nonlinear candidate has direct experimental support. Li et al. found that thin-plate gliders in water and paper planes in air pass, as the CoM moves forward, through flutter, then **bounding** ("bouts of forward gliding interrupted by soft stalls"), then steady gliding, then diving. They likened the gliding-to-bounding transition to a Hopf bifurcation, where a steady glide gives way to a limit cycle ([Li et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2022.89)). Their quasi-steady model placed the boundary too far aft. It *under-predicted* how far forward the CoM must move to stop bounding, which mirrors the project's quasi-steady model predicting decay that did not happen. The aerodynamics supply the mechanism. Thin plates show a lift-slope break near **8° angle of attack**, with the centre of pressure moving aft beyond it ([Okamoto & Jinba](https://akita.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/927/files/KJ00005254825.pdf)). A trim CL of 0.3-0.4 needs roughly 4-6°, leaving only 2-4° of margin to be eaten at the slow crest of each swoop. Free-flight lift is also higher on pitch-up than pitch-down even at very slow pitch rates ([Uhlig & Selig 2013](https://m-selig.web.engr.illinois.edu/pubs/UhligSelig-2013-JofAC-ViconVapor.pdf)), a small hysteresis that can feed energy into the cycle. The seed data fit the same picture. Straight-flying *Alsomitra* split into stable gliders and dynamically unstable bounders, separated by chordwise CoM position ([Certini et al. 2026 dataset](https://datashare.ed.ac.uk/handle/10283/9191)). The project's Zanonia AVL deck puts static margin at only about 1 % MAC at the marked CG (`known_good/zanonia/RESULTS.md`), which is in that zone.

The rival explanation, a lightly damped mode kept alive by gusts, cannot be ruled out from the literature. Outdoor gusts of 0.3-0.5 m/s are 5-10 % of airspeed at frequencies near the phugoid's. A single test separates the two [S]. A limit cycle has a repeatable amplitude whatever the release or the wind, and it persists indoors. A forced response is irregular, tracks the wind, and largely vanishes in still air. The passive cures, ranked, are as follows. First, move the CG forward with matching reflex or decalage [M: Li; H: standard free-flight cure ([Model Aviation, "Where's the CG?"](https://www.modelaviation.com/node/1886))]. Second, lower the trim CL to widen the alpha margin; trimming at CL 0.25-0.3 instead of 0.4 buys about 1.5-2° [A]. Third, add pitch damping through a longer tail arm, which barely changes phugoid damping but should limit overshoot at the crest [S]. Fourth, raise wing loading, which brings higher Re, better L/D and smaller gust ratios; Wiesemüller concluded that heavier payloads were needed to glide straight in wind at all [M/S]. Fifth, chamfer a foamboard leading edge or add slight camber. At t/c ≈ 11 % with a blunt edge, foamboard sits outside every thin-plate dataset, and 5 % circular-arc camber was the best section below Re 70k ([Laitone 1997](https://doi.org/10.1007/s003480050128)) [S, partly M]. The first two cures also serve the lateral robustness goal, which is the most useful convergence in this whole review.

## Passive wing-deployment triggers: stored energy works, airspeed triggers are unproven

Published folding wings on dropped or launched vehicles open by stored energy: torsion springs, elastic hinges, rubber. These are released at launch and sometimes helped by air load. Fujita's 0.9 kg balloon-dropped UAV completed wing deployment and pull-up within about 3 s, even though the right wing opened slightly before the left. His spring hinge could deploy "in any attitude as long as the airspeed is lower than 6.8 m/s," and wind-tunnel tests showed **air load can oppose deployment above ~6.2 m/s**, with easiest opening at 0-15° angle of attack ([Fujita, ICAS 2012](https://icas.org/ICAS_ARCHIVE/ICAS2012/PAPERS/649.PDF)). His one crash came from a stopper that latched on the bench but not in the airflow, so test any lock under flow. Kim's elastic hinges recover slowly (up to 30 s to reach 70 % of nominal geometry) and scatter by 4° ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18861)). Given the lateral sensitivity above, **any folding joint must seat against a hard stop**, never against the hinge's elastic rest angle. Bistable "spring origami" joints exist ([Faber et al. 2018](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/releases/2018/Q1/origami-folds-of-insect-wing-can-help-improve-machine-functions.html)), but the fielded bistable vehicle the team found is triggered by thrust and acceleration, not dynamic pressure ([arXiv 2011.00426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.00426)). The closest working altitude- or time-triggered shape change on a dropped flier is the UW origami microflier, which folds in about 25 ms on a pressure-sensor, timer or Bluetooth trigger ([UW News 2023](https://www.washington.edu/news/2023/09/13/battery-free-robots-use-origami-to-change-shape-in-mid-air/)). By the team's arithmetic, dynamic pressure is only 18 Pa at 5.5 m/s and 60 Pa at 10 m/s. A 20 cm² drag tab would then make 0.04-0.14 N against a glider weight of 0.1-0.2 N, so an airspeed latch would have to be finger-light [A]. The simplest option consistent with the evidence is to let the quad's release clamp also hold the wings folded, so that opening it both drops the glider and frees the panels during the low-lift dive [S].

## Conclusion

The research reframes the goal. "Straight passive flight from any build" has no known solution: not in theory, not in free-flight practice, not in the published micro-glider work. "**Predictable** passive flight from any build" is within reach. It needs three things together: high twist-rejection K from bent-up tips, a lower trim CL that serves pitch and roll at once, and one roll-type trim tab set per airframe by a test glide. Free-flighters reached the same answer decades ago, and they also accept a gentle known-direction circle instead of fighting for a straight line. The decomposition also changes what to fix first. On B45, more fin area would mostly help yaw errors that the AVL study says are minor, while potentially worsening the twist turns that dominate. On Swift, the missing vertical surface and possible tail-bend asymmetry are the likely weak points.

The biggest unknown is calibration, not design. Every lateral rule here was derived for larger, tailed aircraft, and AVL's Clβ is known to be off for thin low-AR plates at this Re. One afternoon of drops with deliberate, measured twist on a single B45 would turn the ranked list from argument into data. It would also give the project the first published warp-to-turn curve at gram scale.
