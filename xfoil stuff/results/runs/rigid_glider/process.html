How we got here, and what the prototype is for

The MMAV glider study as a process, 11 October 2026. Companion to the findings page.

The whole process in seven steps

  1. Done

    Ask one narrow question

    Can a very small glider with no motor and no controls fly steadily by itself, even when it is built and launched imperfectly?

  2. Done

    Pick three ways it might work

    A plain flying wing, a wing with a bent-up lip at the back, and a wing with a small tail. Preferred in that order, because fewer parts fold and stow better.

  3. Done

    Model each one on the computer

    Two standard tools were combined. One works out how the whole wing shares the lift. The other works out how a slice of the wing behaves in slow air. Together they estimate where the glider balances.

  4. Done

    Turn the answer into one number

    How many millimetres the balance point can move before the glider stops gliding. Bigger is more forgiving.

  5. Done

    Have the work checked independently

    Three reviews so far. The last one caught an error in the report and stopped a flat-plate calculation that looked fine but was not trustworthy.

  6. Done

    Decide what to build

    One 400 mm wing that can be flown all three ways. First from flat sheet, then printed with an airfoil shape.

  7. Next

    Fly it and compare with the model

    The flights give the real number from step 4. That tells us which option works and how far the model can be trusted.

The one idea everything rests on: the balance point

A glider is steady only if its weight is balanced a little ahead of the point where the lift acts. Too far forward and it dives. Too far back and it pitches up and stalls. In between there is a band where it settles into a glide. The whole study is about how wide that band is.

A symmetric plain wing has a specific problem: with nothing to push the nose up, it only balances when it is making no lift at all. The lip and the tail both add that small nose-up push, which is why they open the band up.

What the two tools do, and where they fail

Whole-wing tool (AVL)

Treats the air as perfectly slippery. Good at how lift spreads along the span and where the balance-sensitive point is. Blind to everything that depends on air being sticky.

Slice tool (XFOIL)

Looks at one cross section and includes stickiness. At this tiny size the airflow can separate and re-attach abruptly, and the tool's answers jump around when that happens.

What that means

The numbers are good for comparing options and for knowing where to start. They are not good enough to predict a 2 mm band with confidence. That is why the last step is a flight test.

The flat plate is the clearest example. The slice tool gave plate answers that changed with tiny details of the trailing edge and that disagree with wind-tunnel measurements, so they were thrown out.

Is the wing too stubby?

The chosen wing has an aspect ratio of 4.5 (span divided by average width). A slimmer wing glides further, so it is a fair thing to question. Here are the two candidates side by side.

  • For best glide, slimmer wins, but not by much here: roughly 5% on glide ratio (my estimate), because at this size surface drag dominates.
  • The chosen wing is wider on purpose. Wider means the air behaves a little better (higher Reynolds number), and a printed airfoil tip is 3.6 mm thick where the slim wing's would be 2.7 mm.
  • The model cannot separate them. The slim wing is slightly better for the plain flying wing; the wide one is better for the lip and tail.
  • Glide distance is not the goal yet. The question is whether it flies steadily at all. Once that is answered, slimming the wing is an easy second step, and sheet panels make it cheap to try both.

Using the prototype to check the model

Yes, this is exactly what the build is for. One point matters: the model only makes predictions for the printed airfoil wing (NACA 0006). There is no prediction for the flat sheet wing. So the printed wing is the test of the model, and the sheet wing is an experiment in its own right.

Even a rough result is useful. If the real bands are within a few millimetres of these, the model can be trusted to size the next version. If they are far off, we learn which of its assumptions was wrong.

What to do, in order

  • Weigh the printed 20 cm benchmark gliders. Every weight in the model is a guess until then.
  • Print the airfoil wing if the aim is to check the model. Cut the sheet wing as well if time allows; it is quick.
  • Build a simple launcher so every launch is the same.
  • Fly the plain wing first, then the lip, then the tail, and log every launch.
