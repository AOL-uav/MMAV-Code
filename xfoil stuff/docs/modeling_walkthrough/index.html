From AVL to a run you set up yourself

A walkthrough of the modelling process used in this project, written for an engineering student who knows XFOIL and has the ideas of vortex-lattice methods and CFD, but has not set an AVL case up alone. Every screen, file and table quoted below is read from a real file or a real run in this workspace when the page is built (docs/modeling_walkthrough/build_walkthrough.py). Every number in the running text is either computed from those files or checked against them by the build, which stops with an error if one no longer matches; the few exceptions are listed at the end. The runs you can repeat are in docs/modeling_walkthrough/practice/; they are copies, so you cannot damage a project result. About documentation: only the program avl352.exe and a provenance note (tools/avl/README.md) are in the project; the AVL manual is on the MIT AVL page (web.mit.edu/drela/Public/web/avl) and is NOT shipped here. So wherever this page says how AVL behaves, it comes from a run shown here or from AVL's own on-screen menus, and I mark it checked. Things I did not check are said to be unchecked.

2. What a vortex-lattice method is

Start from a picture you already know: a wing makes lift because the air goes faster over the top than under the bottom. A mathematically equivalent way to say it is that the wing carries circulation, a swirl of air around it, and the lift is proportional to that swirl. A vortex-lattice method (VLM) models the swirl directly.

Step 1. Replace the wing by a flat grid

Take the wing outline seen from above. Cut it into a grid of small quadrilateral panels: some number along the chord, some number along the span. The wing is treated as an infinitely thin sheet, so thickness is gone from the start. (That is why the project's AVL files say NACA 0000: zero camber. The thickness digits are not used: checked, practice/review_checks.py ran the F45 deck at CL 0.3 with NACA 0000, 0006 and 0012 in both sections and every printed number (angle, CM, Cma, CLa, Xnp, Clb, Cnb, e) was identical.)

Step 2. One horseshoe vortex per panel

On each panel put a horseshoe vortex: a vortex line across the panel (the bound vortex, about a quarter of the way back from the panel's front edge) whose two ends turn and run straight back to infinity (the trailing legs). Each horseshoe has a strength Gamma (units m²/s), unknown for now. Together the horseshoes are the swirl of the whole wing and its wake.

Step 3. Every vortex pushes air everywhere (Biot-Savart)

A vortex line makes air move around it. The velocity a small piece of vortex line dl, of strength Gamma, causes at a point at vector r from it is

dV = Gamma / (4 pi) * ( dl x r ) / |r|^3          (Biot-Savart law; x is the vector cross product)

In words: stronger vortex, bigger push; farther away, much smaller push (like 1/r² for a small piece, 1/r for a long line); the push is sideways to both the line and the direction to the point. Because the velocity is proportional to Gamma, the total velocity at any point is a plain sum of (known geometry factor) × (unknown Gamma) over all the horseshoes. That is why the maths stays linear.

Step 4. The one condition that is enforced

At one point on each panel, the control point (about three quarters of the way back), the air must not pass through the sheet: the flow component along the panel's normal must be zero. The flow there is the oncoming wind plus the push from every horseshoe:

for every panel i:   sum over j of  A(i,j) * Gamma(j)  =  - V_wind . n(i)

A(i,j) is the normal-direction velocity that horseshoe j makes at control point i for unit Gamma. It depends only on the geometry. So this is N equations in N unknowns, a linear system. The program builds the matrix and solves it. (You can see this in AVL's own output: Building normalwash AIC matrix... Factoring normalwash AIC matrix..., section 5. AIC means aerodynamic influence coefficient: the matrix A.)

Step 5. Forces

Once the Gammas are known, each bound vortex segment feels a force from the Kutta-Joukowski law:

force on a bound vortex segment = rho * ( V x Gamma-vector )     per unit length

Add up all segments for lift, side force and moments about a reference point. The trailing legs carry air downward behind the wing (downwash). That downwash tilts the local flow at the wing and tilts the force backward: that backward part is induced drag. AVL prints it as CDind.

What is left out (read this part twice)

  • Thickness. The wing is a sheet.
  • Viscosity and therefore Reynolds number. There is no boundary layer, no friction drag and no skin-friction dependence on speed. In the real screen output below you will see CDvis = 0.00000.
  • Separation and stall. Nothing in the equations can stop lift growing with angle.

Result: lift rises linearly with angle forever and drag is induced drag only. The measured plates (section 8) reach their first lift peak at: aspect ratio 2: 14 to 16 degrees (the first 'peak' is a flattening of the lift curve in most of the rows: 50 of 52; the largest lift found by searching up to 20 degrees is at 15 to 20 degrees); aspect ratio 3: 11 to 12 degrees (the first 'peak' is a flattening of the lift curve in some of the rows: 8 of 46; the largest lift found by searching up to 20 degrees is at 11 to 19 degrees); aspect ratio 4: 10 to 13 degrees (the first 'peak' is a flattening of the lift curve in some of the rows: 14 of 46; the largest lift found by searching up to 20 degrees is at 10 to 20 degrees); aspect ratio 5: about 10 degrees (the first 'peak' is a flattening of the lift curve in all of the rows: 12 of 12; the largest lift found by searching up to 20 degrees is at 14 to 20 degrees) (columns alpha_first_peak_deg, first_peak_kind, alpha_global_max_to_20deg of step3_summary.csv). The measured plates also have friction and pressure drag that AVL does not see.

How far to trust AVL's neutral point. It is a starting estimate, nothing more. AVL puts the lift of a thin flat sheet at about the quarter chord and never sees the viscous flow over a real plate. The measured plates say the real low-angle aerodynamic centre is not exactly there: in results/runs/rigid_glider/plate_balance/step3_summary.csv (column AC_ahead_of_quarter_chord_pct) the offset runs from -1.6 to 4.8% of chord ahead of the quarter chord over all 156 rows (negative would be behind it). It moves forward as Reynolds number falls: for 10 of the 10 wing shapes the value at the lowest Reynolds number is further forward than at the highest (mean 3.0% at the lowest, 0.8% at the highest). Lower Reynolds number is where these gliders fly, so treat the AVL neutral point as a place to start ballast tests, not a result. AVL's yaw and roll derivatives (Cnb, Clb) have never been checked against anything here: no measurement, no tunnel, no second code. Their signs are checked only against AVL's own runs (section 6), which shows the program is consistent with itself and nothing more. Glide ratio and stall are outside AVL altogether. The project's own cross-check is in section 8.

  • Cref wrong (doubled): CL, alpha, Xnp, Clb, Cnb unchanged, but Cm is halved (Cm is divided by Cref). The neutral point does not care about Cref; the static margin you compute from it would be wrong by the same factor.

7.4 One more habit: which induced drag?

AVL prints two induced-drag numbers (near-field CDind and far-field CDff), see section 5: the near-field value is -2.6% relative to the far-field one there. Lift-gap checks (no CFD): the tunnel's lift excess over AVL

Source: cfd/validation_sweep/lift_gap_checks/ (AVL for all ten tested planforms; the tunnel's low-angle slopes from the plate-balance fits, window −4 to 6°; the thesis wall-correction formulas sized for the AR 3 plate). Across the ten wings the tunnel slope is -0.2% to 30.6% above AVL's (mean 15.2%). The mean excess at each Reynolds number: 5.6% at 60,000, 11.7% at 80,000, 16.5% at 100,000, 21.0% at 120,000; every one of the ten wings rises with Reynolds number, by 1.5 to 2.9 points per 10,000. The thesis wall corrections, sized for the AR 3 plate at Reynolds number 60,000, are about 1.5 to 2.0% of the angle (the constants the thesis takes from figures in its reference 55 are not printed, so bounds were assumed) and about 0.1% in blockage.

Result. The tunnel's lift excess over AVL is not a wall-correction or fixed-offset artefact: it depends on Reynolds number on every wing. No mechanism is named as fact. Caveats:

  • A speed-dependent facility effect is not excluded: changing the Reynolds number in that tunnel changes the speed, and with it the turbulence and the acoustics. Results in this Reynolds-number range differ between tunnels (research/bird_research_notes.md section 6.3, secondary sources).
  • Aeroelastic twist is unlikely but untested: AR 2, the shortest span, has the largest excess.
  • AVL models zero thickness. A 4.3% plate with a rounded nose may carry a few percent more inviscid lift slope, which could account for part of the floor of the excess but not for its trend.
  • The laminar CFD probably lacks a lift-raising viscous effect present in this tunnel at Reynolds number 60,000 (probable, not established). The CFD's own slope shortfall was not measured as a slope, and the refined result is one angle.
  • Below Reynolds number 60,000 this is untested and no number is given. Straight-line extrapolations of the per-wing trends would cross zero at Reynolds numbers between about 12,000 and 65,000 for nine of the ten wings (the tenth does not cross at a positive Reynolds number), so even the sign is unknown there; they are extrapolations, not predictions. The secondary sources disagree (Laitone: more than 20% above lifting-line theory at 40,000; McArthur: no significant disagreement at 10,000 to 20,000). Nothing here says how AVL's lift slope compares with real wings at lower Reynolds numbers.

Where this leaves the 6° and 8° question. The 8° question was about the centre of pressure, not lift, and was decided by running the second refined case above; its result is a PASS on the pre-written rule, on a disclosed shortened window. The lift gap stays open. The rule is unchanged: no CFD has been run on the fabricated wings, and none is used for balance or pull-out, unless Kai decides otherwise.

9. Do it yourself: a checklist for a new shape

  1. Sketch the outline in metres. Top view: x aft from the root leading edge, y out along the wing. Write down the leading-edge point and chord at the root, at every kink, and at the tip.
  2. Compute the reference values. Sref = planform area of the wing (one side times two); Bref = span; Cref = mean aerodynamic chord (area-weighted chord: the integral of chord² over the integral of chord, along the span). Put Xref where you think the CG will be, to start.
  3. Write the file. Copy practice/f45_bare.avl. Change the header numbers, then one SECTION per kink. Keep YDUPLICATE. Keep NACA 0000.
  4. Tail or fins? Add a second SURFACE. For a bend use Ainc = -bend. For a fin, give its two sections different z.
  5. Load it and read the banner. Do the strip and vortex counts match what you meant (section 5)? Fix before running.
  6. Run alpha = 0 and 4. At alpha 0 on a symmetric flat wing without a tail: CL = 0 and Cm = 0. If not, the file is not what you think.
  7. Run CL = 0.3 (A C 0.3). Is the angle plausible (a few degrees)? If it is 10° or 13°, suspect a missing YDUPLICATE or a wrong Sref.
  8. Read Xnp, Cma, CLa; compute the static margin two ways (section 5). They must agree.
  9. Read the signs: Cma < 0, Cnb > 0, Clb < 0 for a stable arrangement (section 3). Zero or the wrong sign is a result, not an error.
  10. Convergence check: rerun with half and twice the panels. Note which numbers move (section 7). Do not quote digits that move.
  11. Write down what AVL cannot say about your shape: stall, drag, Reynolds number, glide ratio. Put it next to your numbers.

Glossary

AIC (aerodynamic influence coefficient)
The matrix element A(i,j): velocity normal to panel i caused by unit-strength horseshoe j.
Aspect ratio (AR)
Span squared divided by wing area.
Biot-Savart law
The velocity a piece of vortex line induces at a point (section 2).
Bound vortex
The part of a horseshoe lying on the wing.
CG (centre of gravity)
The balance point of the glider; AVL takes moments about Xref, which you set to the CG.
Cl_beta (Clb)
Rolling moment per radian of sideslip; negative = stable.
Cm
Pitching moment coefficient about Xref, nose-up positive.
CM0
CM at zero lift.
Cn_beta (Cnb)
Yawing moment per radian of sideslip; positive = weathercock stable.
Control point
The point on a panel where the no-flow-through condition is applied.
Cref, Bref, Sref
Reference chord, span and area used to make coefficients dimensionless.
Downwash
Air pushed downward behind a lifting wing by the trailing vortices.
e (span efficiency)
CDi = CL²/(pi AR e); 1 = elliptic loading.
Horseshoe vortex
One bound vortex plus two trailing legs to infinity.
Induced drag
Drag from the backward tilt of the force caused by downwash.
Kutta-Joukowski law
Force per length on a vortex = density × velocity × circulation.
MAC (mean aerodynamic chord)
Area-weighted mean chord; the length used as Cref.
Neutral point (NP)
The CG position at which the pitching moment stops changing with angle.
Ncrit
XFOIL's transition parameter (how disturbed the free stream is); 5 and 9 are used here.
Panel
One cell of the grid on the wing.
Reynolds number
Speed × chord / kinematic viscosity: how important viscosity is. Tens of thousands for these gliders (not checked here).
Static margin
(x_np - x_cg)/Cref; positive = CG ahead of the NP = stable in pitch.
Strip
A spanwise row of panels; AVL reports lift per strip.
Trailing leg
The part of a horseshoe that runs back from the wing.
Trefftz plane
A plane far behind the wing where AVL evaluates induced drag (far field).
Trim
A state where the moments about the CG sum to zero.
VLM
Vortex-lattice method.
YDUPLICATE
AVL keyword: mirror this surface about the y = 0 plane.
